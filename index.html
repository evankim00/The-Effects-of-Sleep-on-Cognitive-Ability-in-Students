Cognitive effects of sleep loss in students

Two high school students take the same classes for one 13-week semester. Student A sleeps 8 hours a night. Student B sleeps 5. Based on published sleep research, this simulator shows how that gap builds up and changes how they feel, react and perform in class.

How the simulation works

Every number on this page comes from the equations below. The studies set the direction and rough size of each effect; the exact coefficients are our assumptions, chosen to stay in a believable range. Treat the output as an illustration, not a prediction.

Sleep debt

Dt = max(0, Dt−1 + 8 − st)

st is the hours slept the night before day t.

Short-term sleep pressure

Pt = Pt−1·e−1/4 + (8 − st)

Fades over about 4 nights, so two good nights don't erase it1.

Long-term load

St = St−1·e−1/21 + (8 − st)

Builds up and fades over about 3 weeks6.

Sleepiness

KSS = 3 + 6·(1 − e−P/12)

Starts at 3 (alert) and levels off below 9.

Reaction time

plapse = 0.01 + 0.0015·S + 0.003·P
RT = (1 − p)(255 + 1.2·S + 1.5·P) + p·650 ms

A lapse is a response of 500 ms or more4.

Daily score

score = 96 − m·40·(1 − e−(0.3·S + 0.4·P)/40) ± 2.5

m = 1.15 for Biology, 1.00 for U.S. History, 0.85 for Algebra II.

Limitations

  • The lab studies lasted 5–7 nights. Extending their trends across 13 weeks is an extrapolation.
  • Real grades depend on much more than sleep: effort, teaching, health, stress, caffeine and luck.
  • People respond to sleep loss differently. These are two made-up students, not averages.
